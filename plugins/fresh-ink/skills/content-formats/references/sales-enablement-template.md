# Sales enablement scaffolds

Three assets, one job: give the sales team language they can repeat accurately on a live call. Each asset has to be useful to a rep who skims it between calls: benefit-led, honest about where the product loses, and short enough to scan in under a minute.

Competitor facts (positioning, pricing, weak spots) aren't bundled here. Bring them from your team's research, verify them, and date them. Ask the buyer's role, seniority, and what they already know before you write anything.

---

## Rules for all three assets

- **Lead with the outcome.** A feature alone means nothing to a buyer. Pair it with what it does for them: "the code runs in the browser, so your security team has no server to review" is the benefit.
- **Admit the losses.** Every battle card needs a section on where the competitor is the better choice. Reps who've been oversold stop trusting the card.
- **Write it so it can be said aloud.** If a line can't be spoken on a call without sounding like an ad, rewrite it as speech.
- **Speak the buyer's decision language.** Speed, cost, governance, risk, time to value.
- **One idea per asset.** A one-pager has one positioning idea. A battle card has one competitor. An objection handler has one format.
- **Date every competitive fact.** Note when it was true, so reps know when to refresh.

---

## Asset 1: Battle card (one named competitor)

One card per competitor, one screen. Keep it scannable in under a minute.

**Before drafting:** which competitor, and for which buyer (an agency buyer weighs margin and client handoff; an in-house team weighs brand control and launch dates). Pull current pricing and positioning from your research and note the date. Collect real proof: outcomes, metrics, accounts you can reference.

### Sections

1. **Snapshot (four to six bullets).** What the competitor sells, to whom, how it's priced, its real strength, and its known weak spots. Fair enough that a rep trusts it.
2. **Where we win (three to five points).** Each one a capability followed by the outcome it produces for this buyer.
3. **Where they win (two or three points).** Real edges, with the situations where they're the right call, and what a rep should concede without a fight.
4. **Landmines (two to four points).** Claims not to make, comparisons that backfire, features not to oversell.
5. **Discovery questions (four to six).** Open questions that reveal fit and surface the competitor's gaps without trashing anyone.
6. **Proof (three to five items).** Outcomes, named accounts, cited third-party figures, each dated.
7. **One-line positioning.** One sentence framing us against this competitor. True, memorable, repeatable.

## Asset 2: Objection handler

A format, applied to the objections reps hear most. The format is what reps keep: once they've got it, they can handle objections the card doesn't list.

For each objection, fill five slots:

1. **Objection:** what the buyer says, in their words.
2. **Underlying concern:** what's really behind it, which is often not the stated objection.
3. **Reframe:** shift from the worry to the relevant truth. Acknowledge first. Never dismiss.
4. **Proof:** the capability, metric, account, or cited figure that backs the reframe.
5. **Line to say:** one breath, verbatim.

**Example shapes** (illustrative; replace with real objections and verified proof):

- **"Is the output production-ready, or just a demo?"** Underlying concern: they've inherited prototypes that couldn't be turned into real code. Reframe: most tools stop at the demo, and ours hands over code the team can keep. Proof: the export or integration that makes that true, with a customer who shipped it. Line: "Fair concern. The output is real code in your repo, so engineers refine it rather than rebuild it. Want to see the handoff?"
- **"What about security?"** Underlying concern: a review will take weeks, and nobody wants unsanctioned tools in the building. Reframe: the architecture simplifies the review. Proof: the specific design choice and the documents that back it. Line: "That's the right question. Our architecture is built for that review, and I can send the documentation today so you can start."
- **"We already use a competitor."** Underlying concern: tool sprawl and overlapping spend. Reframe: the two tools may cover different jobs, which is the honest case. Proof: the integration or the division of work, stated plainly. Line: "Keep your current tool for the work it does well. This covers the requests that currently land on your engineers' backlog."

## Asset 3: Competitive one-sheet

A single page making the standalone case for us to one buyer. Less combat than the battle card; more a clear statement of why we're the choice.

**Before drafting:** name the buyer (role, seniority, what they know). Pick the one takeaway the page must leave. Collect the proof that matters to this buyer.

### Sections

1. **Positioning (two or three sentences).** What we are, who for, and the core promise, in the buyer's language. Lead with the outcome.
2. **Differentiators (three to five points).** Each a capability tied to a result the buyer cares about. Pick differentiators that hold up whether or not a competitor is in the room.
3. **Proof (three to five items).** Lead with the strongest number.
4. **Target buyer (two to four sentences).** The persona, the trigger that makes them look for a tool like ours, and the moment we become relevant. This tells the rep who to hand the sheet to.

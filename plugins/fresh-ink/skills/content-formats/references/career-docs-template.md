# Career docs and pitches

Structural specs for the documents that sell a person rather than a product: cover letter, resume bullets, bio, LinkedIn About, and a pitch to an editor. Voice is the **professional register** in tone-and-guidelines (Register 2). craft fills these shapes; this file only defines them.

## The truth gate (all five documents)

Before drafting, build a **proof bank** from the user's source material (resume, portfolio, past bios, LinkedIn, notes): one line per accomplishment, each with a number, a scale, or a named artifact. Every claim in the finished document maps to a line in the bank.

- Mirror the target's language only where it's true of the user. If the job description says "Contentful" and the user has used Contentful, say Contentful. If they haven't, don't.
- Never add a skill, tool, credential, title, or result the source material doesn't support. When a requirement has no proof, flag it to the user as a gap and ask; don't stretch.
- Phrase partial matches honestly: "I use Chicago for citations and have worked in AP" is stronger than a claim that falls apart in the interview.

## Cover letter

**Length:** 250 to 400 words, four or five paragraphs. One page, always.

**Before drafting:** pull the job description's top five requirements, ranked by how often and how early they appear. Map each to a proof-bank line, or mark it as a gap.

| Section | Job | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Opening (2 to 3 sentences) | Why this role, at this company, from this person | Lead with a specific: a result, a piece of their work, a problem they've named publicly. Never "I am writing to apply for." |
| Proof paragraph 1 | The requirement they care about most, answered with the strongest proof | Result first, then how. One number minimum. |
| Proof paragraph 2 | The second and third requirements | Can combine if the proof overlaps. |
| The fit (optional) | Why the user's specific path matters to this team | Only if it's true and specific. Skip it rather than pad. |
| Close (2 sentences) | A forward-looking line and the ask | No "thank you for your consideration" filler; one plain thank-you is fine. |

**Fails:** restating the resume in paragraphs, a generic opener that could go to any company, adjectives about yourself with no proof ("passionate," "results-driven," "detail-oriented"), more than one weak beat of humor.

## Resume bullets

**Pattern:** strong verb + what you did + the measurable result + scale or timeframe. "Grew organic traffic 340% in 14 months by rebuilding the blog around 60 bottom-funnel queries."

- 3 to 5 bullets per role; the most recent role gets the most.
- One line each where possible, two at most.
- Lead with the result when it's the impressive part: "Cut time to publish from 9 days to 3 by..."
- Tailor the order, not the facts: for each target, move the bullets that answer its top requirements to the top of their role.
- Mirror the job description's nouns (tools, methods, titles) where they're true, so applicant tracking systems match them.
- **Banned:** "responsible for," "helped to," "worked on," "various," "duties included," first-person pronouns, and any number the user can't defend.

**Summary line (optional, top of resume):** two sentences: who they are professionally and the one result that proves it. No objective statements.

## Bio

Write three lengths at once, so the user has the right one when someone asks:

| Length | Words | Use | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short | 25 to 40 | Speaker cards, bylines, social | Name, role, one credential, one human detail |
| Medium | 75 to 100 | Conference programs, guest posts | Adds two proof points and what they're working on now |
| Long | 150 to 200 | Website About, press kits | Adds the arc: where they came from and why it matters to this audience |

- Third person by default; first person if the user asks or the venue is personal (their own site, a newsletter).
- Lead with the role and the credential, not the life story.
- One human detail (a hobby, a place, an obsession) makes the bio stick. Make it specific.

## LinkedIn About

**Length:** 150 to 300 words. First person. The first two lines show before "see more," so they carry the whole pitch.

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| Hook (lines 1 to 2) | What they do and for whom, or the problem they solve, in plain words |
| Proof (1 paragraph) | Two or three results with numbers |
| How they work (1 paragraph) | The point of view or method that makes them different |
| What they're looking for or open to | Roles, projects, collaborations; be specific |
| Contact line | How to reach them |

Keywords recruiters search for (job titles, tools, specialties) belong in the text naturally, not in a list at the bottom.

## Pitch to an editor (op-ed, guest post, story)

**Length:** 150 to 300 words, in the body of an email. No attachments unless asked.

| Part | Job |
|---|---|
| Subject line | The headline, or the angle in under 10 words. Run headline-lab if it matters. |
| Line 1 | The news hook or the argument, stated plainly. Why now. |
| The angle (2 to 4 sentences) | What the piece argues and what it adds that this outlet hasn't published |
| Why this writer | One or two credentials that make the user the right person to write it |
| Logistics | Proposed length, timing, whether a draft exists, and a link to two relevant clips |

- Research the outlet first: its recent pieces on the topic, its guest guidelines, and the right editor. Never pitch a piece they ran last month.
- One pitch, one idea. A list of three possible angles reads as no angle.
- For op-eds, the argument goes in the first sentence. Editors decide in that sentence.

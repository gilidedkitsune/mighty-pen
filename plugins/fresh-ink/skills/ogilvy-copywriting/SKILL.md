# Ogilvy copywriting

A persuasion check. Copy with a sales goal gets judged on one thing: did it move the reader toward the action. Everything below serves that. Use it before drafting to set the strategy, and after drafting to find what fails.

---

## Order of decisions

Each decision depends on the ones above it. A sharp headline can't rescue a wrong position.

### 1. Positioning

Positioning answers two questions: what does this do, and for whom? Settle it before writing a line. Examples of the idea (illustrative): a bar of soap positioned as a skin treatment for people with dry skin, not as something that cleans; a mixer positioned as the thing that makes a drink, not as a soft drink.

Target a mindset, not a demographic. "People who distrust corporate software" tells a writer more than "ages 25 to 45."

### 2. The promise

The promise is one specific benefit the buyer gets, and the product can actually deliver it. It is not a slogan. Most advertising promises nothing, which is why it fails.

- A benefit, not a feature.
- Unique to this product, or at least stated better than competitors state it.
- True, and provable.
- **One promise.** Strategy documents that carry three promises produce copy that carries none. Pick the strongest and deliver it completely.

### 3. Brand image

Each piece adds to a cumulative identity. Most advertising is produced one campaign at a time with no common thread, which wastes the cumulative effect. A consistent personality over years is what builds share. Visual quality is part of the message: a sloppy ad tells the reader the product is sloppy.

### 4. The big idea

Without one, the work disappears. A big idea interrupts indifference and makes the reader remember. Big ideas are usually simple, and they usually take real homework: knowing the product, studying what has worked and failed in the category, and understanding what buyers think, want, and fear. A strong idea can run unchanged for years.

To find it:
1. Learn the product deeply.
2. Study what has and hasn't worked in this category.
3. Read what buyers actually say (see craft's voc-message-mining).
4. Pick the promise that research says is most persuasive, and make the idea carry that promise.

## Headlines

Most readers who see an ad read its headline and stop. Treat the headline as the largest share of the budget.

- **Carry the brand and the promise.** A reader should know who's speaking and what they get without reading further.
- **Promise a benefit.** Benefit headlines beat feature headlines in test after test.
- **Bring news if there is any.** Readers scan for what's new. A plain announcement of something new beats a clever line about something old.
- **Use plain language.** Nobody decodes a headline. State the message in the words the reader uses.
- **Length follows the job.** Recall-driven headlines run about 8 to 10 words. Response-driven headlines can run 6 to 12, and in direct response a long, specific headline often beats a short clever one. A real example: "At 60 miles an hour, the loudest noise in this new car comes from the clock." It works because every word is a fact.
- **Name the audience when there is one.** "For first-time home buyers" selects its own readers.
- **Avoid negatives.** A headline that starts with "Don't" or "Never" loses readers in testing.
- **Curiosity earns reads only if the body pays it off.** A gap in the headline that the copy never closes is a broken promise.

---

## Body copy

- **More information sells more, up to a point.** Readership drops sharply over the first 50 words and then flattens for long-form readers who are already interested. Long copy suits interested buyers; short copy suits everyone else.
- **Write for an intelligent reader.** Condescension loses people who could have bought.
- **Use facts.** Specific, relevant facts persuade. Adjectives ("amazing," "revolutionary") don't; they assert what the facts should prove.
- **Be useful.** Advice, information, or a practical tool beyond the pitch holds readers longer and builds trust for the sale.

---

## Visuals and layout

- **Photographs beat illustrations** on attention, believability, and recall, when the photo is the product or the situation the product solves.
- **Captions get read.** More people read a photo's caption than the body copy beneath it. Every caption should work as a small ad with the brand and the promise in it.
- **Story appeal stops the scroll.** An image that makes the viewer ask "what's happening here?" pulls them into the copy.
- **Before-and-after contrasts** earn above-average attention when the change is real and measurable.
- **Editorial layouts** read more like content and get less resistance than layouts that look like advertising.
- **Ugly work reads as a cheap product.**
- **Repeat what works.** Good ads are often pulled before they've paid back. Repetition up to several exposures can raise recall. Don't retire a winner because the team is bored with it.

---

## Video

- **Open with energy.** Strong openings hold attention; quiet ones lose it.
- **Testimonials work** when the speaker is credible and relevant. An irrelevant celebrity pulls attention away from the product.
- **Problem, then solution, then proof.** Show a problem the viewer recognizes, show the product solving it, and prove the result. Viewers notice faked proof.
- **Show the promise working.** A demonstration makes the claim concrete and easier to remember.
- **A person on camera outperforms a voiceover** on credibility measures.
- **Background music lowers recall.** It's counterintuitive and it holds up.
- **Give the ad a memorable marker.** A recurring sound, image, or phrase helps a viewer who's seen twenty ads that day remember yours.
- **Live action persuades adults better than animation.** Animation works for children.
- **Don't drown the viewer in words.** Let the picture carry the story; narration explains what the picture can't show.

---

## Common failures

- **Chasing awards.** Creative prizes don't track with sales. Good persuasion makes the product the hero, not the ad.
- **Copying trends or competitors.** Imitation rarely wins. Test new approaches before committing to them.
- **Boredom.** Cold, impersonal copy persuades nobody. Talk like a person and aim for a feeling.
- **Burying the news.** A launch is the easiest moment to earn attention, and many teams hedge it into the footnotes.
- **Over-complication.** One strategy, one promise, one idea. Committees dilute copy by adding objectives until nothing is left.

---

## Diagnostic questions

Use these as a review on a finished draft. Fix what fails; leave what works alone.

1. **Positioning:** what does this do, and for whom? Could a reader say it back?
2. **Promise:** is there exactly one, and is it the one the copy delivers?
3. **Idea:** is there a simple, memorable idea carrying the promise, or just a list of benefits?
4. **Reader:** is the reader named in mindset terms, not only in demographics?
5. **Headline:** does it carry the brand, the promise, and any news?
6. **Proof:** where are the facts? Which claims rest on adjectives instead?
7. **Image and caption:** does the visual support the promise, and does the caption work alone?
8. **Interest:** would the copy make the reader lean in, or drift off?
9. **Product:** is the product the hero, or has the cleverness taken over?

## Quick reference

| Element | What tends to work |
|---|---|
| Positioning | A mindset, not a demographic |
| Promise | One specific, provable benefit |
| Headline | Brand, promise, and news in plain words; 8 to 12 words for recall |
| Body length | Long for interested buyers, short for quick decisions |
| Visuals | Photographs of the real thing, captioned |
| Layout | Editorial over advertising-looking |
| Video | Strong opening, real people, visible proof, no background music |
| Repetition | Keep winners running longer than feels comfortable |
| Tone | Human, specific, and intelligent |
| Scope | One promise, delivered fully |

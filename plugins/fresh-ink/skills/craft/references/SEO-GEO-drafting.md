# Writing for search and AI answers

The drafting rules for copy that has to rank in Google and be quoted by AI answer engines (ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI Overviews, Gemini, Copilot). Applies to blog, long-form, and website copy. It sits on top of tone-and-guidelines and no-slops and doesn't replace either.

This file covers writing only: how the words earn rankings and citations. Checking a finished draft against these rules is search-weave's job. Keyword research, SERP analysis, audits, data pulls, and AI-visibility measurement belong to the Insights plugin, which is optional and dormant until connected.

---

## The core pattern

Search engines and AI answer engines reward the same structure, so you don't write two versions. Build the piece as a question tree of self-contained, answer-first sections. Open each section with a short, neutral, sourced answer full of first-hand specifics. Expand underneath with argument, nuance, and depth in plain language.

Nearly every SEO rule also serves AI citation. Only three tensions remain, and each is settled inside a section, not across the whole page (see "Three tensions" below).

---

## The shared spine

### 1. Answer-first, self-contained sections (the keystone)
Each H2 and H3 fully answers the question its heading asks. Open with the answer in the first sentence or two, roughly 40 to 60 words, then expand with evidence and detail. Restate the subject at the top of each section. Don't write "as mentioned above" or let pronouns reach back into an earlier section. Test: if this section were the only thing on the page, or screenshotted alone, would it answer its heading? Ranking, snippets, and AI extraction all lift sections out of context, so a section that leans on its neighbors can't be ranked or quoted.

### 2. Headers as the reader's questions
Phrase headers as the question a person would type or ask, in their words. Keep the hierarchy clean (H1, then H2, then H3, no skipped levels). Typical openings: "What is," "How does," "Why," "Which is best," "Should I."

### 3. Specific, sourced claims (the strongest lever)
Replace every vague claim with a number, a unit, a context, and a source. One habit covers search's experience signal and the strongest citation levers, which are statistics, quotations, and sources.

- Vague: "Websites can get expensive." "Studies show AI improves productivity."
- Specific: "A small-business site from an agency typically costs $8,000 to $30,000 in 2026." "In our 2026 onboarding of about 40 B2B accounts, imports failed most often at the data-mapping step."

Attribute each claim in the same sentence, using the two-part citation defined in tone-and-guidelines (Bibliography and attribution): the claim's key phrase hyperlinked to the original source, then a plain "(Publisher, year)" closing the sentence. That plain parenthetical is what survives when an engine strips links and lifts the passage.

### 4. Information gain
Each section should add something that isn't already common knowledge online: an original observation, a real workflow, an actual cost breakdown, an honest tradeoff, a specific before and after. A section anyone could write without using the product adds nothing and earns nothing.

### 5. Entities first, natural language
Write about the named things (products, companies, concepts), not search phrases. Use full proper names on first mention in each section and tie each one to its category ("[Product], a [category]..."). Cover the related entities and subtopics a knowledgeable writer would naturally include. Keyword density has no value for search or AI; stuffing is the worst option. Aim for about a seventh-to-ninth-grade reading level.

### 6. Committed claims
Take a position where the evidence supports one. Extracted text tends toward definitive statements, and hedged "some say X, others say Y" passages give an engine nothing to quote and a reader nothing to trust. Hedge only where the uncertainty is real, and accuracy always wins.

### 7. Structures that lift cleanly
AI engines quote these almost verbatim, and readers scan them:
- Comparison tables, each preceded by one plain sentence saying what it shows.
- Bullets where every bullet is a complete statement, not a fragment that needs the stem.
- Numbered steps for any process.
- FAQ blocks of real questions, each answered in 40 to 60 words with one intent.

### 8. Dates in the prose
State dates in the sentence itself ("as of 2026") and use current-year figures. Living pages get a visible "last updated" line and a one-line changelog. When a page is refreshed, update the numbers, not just the date, and never fake a date. AI engines rotate toward fresh content faster than traditional search does.

## Three tensions (settle them inside each section)

Most SEO rules also serve GEO. These three trade-offs remain, and each is resolved within a section:

1. **Tone.** Lead each section with a neutral, factual answer, since that's what gets quoted and shown as a snippet. Put brand voice and persuasion below it. Promotional tone at the top measurably reduces citation.
2. **Length.** Be thorough at the page level and brief at the passage level: many self-contained answer blocks under question headers, not one long undivided essay.
3. **Citation density.** Lean on sourced statistics, quotations, and citations more than old SEO habits suggest, because they're the strongest citation levers. Every one needs attribution and a natural place in the text. The target is specific and sourced, not stuffed with figures.

## Engine notes

Engines overlap less than people assume in what they cite, so write for retrieval and extraction, not one blue-link ranking.

- **ChatGPT:** favors an encyclopedic tone and established entities. Put the answer first.
- **Perplexity:** searches live for each query and weights freshness heavily. Be the cleanest source to extract from, and match conversational phrasing.
- **Google AI Overviews:** pulls tight, standalone passages. Strip promotional tone. Question-shaped subheads with statistics do well.
- **Gemini:** treat it like AI Overviews until better data exists.
- **Copilot:** draws on Bing's index, but selection isn't pure rank. Structure and readability carry more weight.

---

## Review cadence

- **Durable (quarterly):** the spine (sections 1 to 8), the entity approach, expertise shown in the prose, specificity, committed claims, and the liftable structures.
- **Fast-moving (monthly):** the engine notes, the exact figures (the 40 to 60 word answer, passage length, snippet sizes, freshness windows), and any conventions for machine-readable pages. This is the layer to re-verify most often.
